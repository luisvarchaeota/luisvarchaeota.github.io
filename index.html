01 / Research

From microbial communities
to molecular chemistry.

01

Communities and mobile genetic elements

I study bacteria, archaea, and their mobile genetic elements as interacting parts of microbial ecosystems. Genome reconstruction and gene-expression analyses help me investigate how metabolism, gene exchange, and ecological interactions shape community structure.

My work spans archaea in wetland soils and biofilms sustained by deeply sourced groundwater, connecting microbial diversity to carbon, sulfur, and hydrogen cycling. I am interested in how these interactions influence biofilm formation and the organization of microbial communities.

Microbial ecology / Mobile genetic elements / Biofilms

Archaea in wetland soils Groundwater-supported biofilms Mobile genetic elements in archaeal genomes
02

Enzyme diversity and innovation

Life has evolved for at least 3.5 billion years, generating an extraordinary diversity of proteins. Yet many remain functionally unannotated or lack an experimentally established function. I connect environmental genomics, evolutionary analysis, and biochemistry to uncover what these proteins do and how their activities support life in natural environments.

Our work on archaeal hydrogenases revealed active minimal and hybrid enzymes, expanding the known diversity of hydrogen metabolism. These discoveries motivate my broader interest in how evolution generates new enzyme architectures, reactions, and biological roles.

Protein function / Enzyme evolution / Biochemistry

Minimal and hybrid hydrogenases Methyltransferase diversity
03

Chemical signals in microbial defense

In the Doudna Lab, I investigate where defense signals originate and what happens to the nucleic acid fragments produced during an immune response. I combine biochemistry, bacterial genetics, and structural biology to explore new chemical mechanisms, including tRNA as a potential source of second messengers.

By tracing how these molecules are generated, recognized, transformed, and cleared, I aim to understand how cells activate defense and control its duration. Resolving this chemistry could reveal how microbes balance protection with survival and provide a foundation for new molecular tools.

03 / About

Discovery across scales.

I am a postdoctoral scholar in Jennifer Doudna’s lab at the Innovative Genomics Institute, UC Berkeley, where I started in September 2026. I investigate the origins and fates of chemical signals in microbial defense, connecting evolutionary questions with biochemistry and structural biology.

Previously, I was a Research Fellow and then Group Leader at Monash University, with my appointment ending in August 2026. I received an HFSP Long-Term Fellowship in 2025 and completed my PhD in Microbiology at UC Berkeley in 2024, with research mentors Jillian Banfield and David Savage.

My research connects ecosystem-scale genomics with enzyme function to understand how bacteria, archaea, and mobile genetic elements shape microbial communities. I am particularly interested in combining AI for biology with structural bioinformatics to uncover uncharacterized enzyme functions and understand RNA-guided systems, gene regulation, and prokaryotic defense. By integrating genomics and evolutionary analysis with biochemistry and structural biology, I aim to connect computational predictions with experimental evidence to reveal molecular mechanisms—from carbon and energy metabolism in wetland soils and biofilms to nucleic acid signaling in microbial defense.

04 / Teaching & learning

Sharing the tools of discovery.

I have held an instructor appointment at Contra Costa College since 2022 and have returned to teaching General Microbiology following my move back to Berkeley. I also served as a graduate student instructor in microbiology and biochemistry at UC Berkeley. My teaching connects core biological principles with questions in health, the environment, and biotechnology.

I have mentored undergraduate and graduate researchers, including doctoral co-supervision and advisory-panel service at Monash through August 2026. I also contributed to the Bioinformatics Virtual Coordination Network, an open-source, interactive learning environment.
